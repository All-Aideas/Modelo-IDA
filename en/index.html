The model in four lines

Effective automation = what AI can technically do, minus what legally needs a human signature, times how well the AI actually performs. Always less than the brochure promises.

Continuity floor (P′) = the number of people you must keep to run the critical operation the day the AI is unavailable. You never go below it, no matter how much you automate — and that is the point most business cases miss.

Monthly saving (S) = the salaries you genuinely stop paying, minus the AI bill adjusted by architecture risk (λ), minus maintenance. It counts released salaries, not automated hours: automating 3.84 people-equivalents when you can only release 3 saves you 3 salaries, not 3.84.

IDA = how much you delegated, times how fragile the thing holding it up is, discounted by whoever outside the area could actually cover for you. Zero to 100. It deliberately contains no money: if savings entered the index, a big saving would mask the risk exactly when you are most exposed.

Automation

All three are explained with the same example: a gas utility that wants to automate its email complaint handling. Open each one to see it.

60%
Example: the gas utility's emails
The team receives complaints by email all day. You look at what comes in and see most are repeat questions: when the technician is coming, how to read the bill, why it came in higher, how to request a change of account holder. The AI drafts those without trouble.

The rest it cannot do: dispatching a crew, interpreting a photo of a broken meter, approving a refund. If counting them gives you 6 out of every 10 hours of the team going to the repetitive part, your a = 60%.

The question is "can it?", not "should we?" This field is what is technically possible; what cannot be delegated by regulation goes in the next one.
20%
Example: the gas utility's emails
Among those same emails, there are some that cannot be answered by a machine even if technically they could: the one saying "I smell gas", the one announcing legal action, the one disputing a disconnection with debt involved. A qualified person has to step in, and there is a record of who answered.

Si eso es 1 de cada 5, tu r = 20%.

Mind the difference: a is what the AI puede do; r is what it is not allowed to do. In banking or healthcare, r is not estimated: you read it from the regulation.
80%
Example: the gas utility's emails
It is not how clever the AI is: it is how much finished work it delivers.

Still with the emails. You hand it 10 complaints it is allowed to answer. It comes back with all 10 replies written, but on review you see that 2 have to be redone: one quotes the wrong account number, another promises a visit on the wrong day. Someone had to read them, spot it and fix it.

So it did not perform like a person: it performed like 8 de cada 10 → η = 80%.

That is why it is never 100%: someone is always reviewing. If nobody reviews, η did not go up — what went up is the risk of sending a customer something wrong.
All three together, same case
The gas utility started out thinking it was automating 60% of the work. But of that 60%, 20% needs a human signature, and of what is left the AI delivers 8 out of 10:

0,60 × (1 − 0,20) × 0,80 = 0,384 → 38,4%

That 38.4% is the real number to do the maths with, not the 60% of the initial promise.

What counts and what does not
This is the easiest number to inflate. It is not the company headcount. Gasto mensual en IA: $129  ·  your automated hours: 614 h/mes

Why we ask per month and not per hour. An expensive model usually needs fewer tokens for the same task, and a cheap one more. Asking "how many tokens per hour" would force you to guess that trade-off; asking for your monthly spend includes it automatically. Also, "an hour of work" is not a full hour of a person — that includes meetings, calls and a hundred things the AI does not do: these are the hours you already counted as automatable above.

Poner 0 for the sysadmin is a valid answer if a tech lead already absorbs the task. What is not valid is not having thought about it: it is the hidden cost that sinks the most on-premise AI projects.

What belongs here
Vendor or consultancy fees, your own developers' hours, integration, data migration, initial licences, and your staff's hours defining rules and reviewing output. Hardware only if you buy it — if you rent it, it belongs in the monthly cost.

Internal hours are not free just because they are internal: they are usually 20–30% of the total. Counting them as zero turns your payback into fiction.
Why 18, and when to change it
18 months is the default because in AI that is an eternity: two years out, the token price, the model you use and even the provider have changed. A project that takes longer than that to return the money is being paid for with assumptions that will no longer exist.

Pero the horizon is yours, not the model's. A bank assessing critical infrastructure works with 36-month horizons without blinking; a startup burning cash cannot take 12. Set it to the horizon your company actually approves investments with: it changes the payback verdict and it changes the investment ceiling, which is saving × this horizon.

What counts as a "contingency plan"? See examples

It is not a document in a drawer. A real contingency plan has four things, and if you are missing any, tick the box:

1 · The written manual procedure. Step by step of how the work was done before the AI: which system to open, what to check, who to notify. If the only person who knows is "the one who has been here since the start", there is no plan.

2 · People genuinely trained, not just listed. This is the key part: it is not enough to tener the people. If the AI has been doing everything for eight months and they only review output, they have lost the practice. The day the AI is gone they will not be able to start, and they will have to relearn under pressure, with the business at a standstill.

3 · Regular drills. The way to solve the above: once a quarter, switch the AI off on purpose for a few hours and operate by hand. Same idea as a fire drill. It is the only way to know whether your continuity floor is real or just a number in a spreadsheet. It costs hours, and those hours are part of the insurance.

4 · Un segundo camino técnico probado. Another provider with credentials already loaded and tested, or a smaller model running locally that can carry the critical load. Tested: if you never switched it on, it does not count.

The most common mistake is having point 1 and believing that is enough. A procedure nobody has executed in a year is not a plan: it is a piece of paper. If that is your situation, leave the box ticked — your risk is real.
